Ballanheim is a queer upcycling label from Berlin run by Andrew, who turns discarded clothes into one-off pieces. I shot the first campaign, built the identity around a single struck seal and wrote the website, so everything the label puts out feels like its own.

Andrew works at the local Humana, which is also one of his main sources of inspiration. He draws a lot from the people who put together a whole look from worn second-hand pieces.

It’s not just the style that makes Ballanheim stand out, but the vision behind it too. Everything he makes is made from recycled materials.

When I interviewed him for the website, I found out that he wanted to create something closely connected to his Scottish heritage, but also something that pushes back against the old way of doing things. That kind of attitude feels pretty familiar in Berlin.

For Andrew, clothes should have no gender, feel as comfortable as possible and work just as well on the street as they do on the dancefloor. He brings a fresh energy to traditional patterns from his home country.

For the campaign, he wanted to use daylight and only bring out the flash when it got too dark. The idea was to keep everything raw.

The project also taught me how to find a compromise with a client and how to have the conversations that help you understand what they actually want.

bleibt is a brand concept I came up with for Berlin bars: five sodas served in a heavy glass jar with an aluminium twist lid. The bartender pours 4 cl of spirit up to a line etched into the glass, and the long drink is ready. The guest gets to take the glass home.

People often choose a drink because it looks good and fits their style, not necessarily because it tastes the best. In Germany, true fruits sells smoothies in glass bottles, along with shakers and pourers that encourage people to keep the empty bottles. When I was a kid, I loved Voss water because of its clear glass cylinder. I never really cared about the water itself.

With bleibt, I wanted to make the glass part of the product, not just something the drink comes in.

Since 2023, German cafés and bars selling drinks to go have had to offer a reusable option, and in 2025, only 61% of Gen Z in Germany drank alcohol. A soda that works with or without the 4 cl fits that audience, while a glass people can keep makes the idea reusable in a simple, everyday way.

The idea is to sell bleibt by the crate to bars in Neukölln, Kreuzberg and Friedrichshain, as well as cafés that turn into bars at night and Spätis. The target audience is the kind of urban creative the Sinus Institute calls Expeditives, around one in ten Germans, followed by Performers and the Neo-Ecological milieu.

The jar is 100 mm wide, 82 mm tall and holds 360 ml. I shaped it like a cosmetics jar so it feels more like an object than a regular drinks bottle. It holds 200 ml of soda, and the line 60 mm from the base marks where the extra 4 cl goes. The brushed aluminium lid has a seal to keep the soda fizzy and make the drink easy to take away.

The narrow 28 mm label only shows the flavour. Each one is named after a moment in a Berlin night: Second Date, Raucherraum, Golden Hour, After Hours and Soundcheck. The brand sits on a paper strip across the lid, which tears when you first open it. The spray-painted grain of the wordmark runs through all the labels.

The idea came from a conversation with a friend of mine who is also from Poland. We realised how differently people read the two of us through gender, and how often she gets seen as the woman from the East.

I wanted the shoot to feel a little messy and play with that image. She’s the gangster’s girlfriend, and the gangster shows up too, wearing a balaclava.

The colours come from the Polish flag: white and red.

Her poses are provocative and free. They don’t need to mean anything beyond her being sexual. Nothing to add, nothing to take away.

I worked on the styling, art direction, photography and editing.

Zola, whose designs you can see in the photos, was looking for a photographer who could capture a bit of queer, carefree joy in her parents’ fabric factory.

She grew up surrounded by fashion and has always known she wanted to make something unapologetically different.

Her clothes are weird and unusual in the best way, and reflect the energy she’s found in Berlin over the past few years.

There aren’t many pictures here, but I think they capture what we were going for: a wild girl who needs no one and is on the hunt.

On a freezing day in the middle of the Berlin winter, we spent hours figuring out how to show the strong woman she is.

I wanted to capture her waiting for prey, with an expression closer to a wild animal than to a subtle, fragile girly girl.

Ulia is still one of my role models. She’s one of the strongest and most creative people I know, and always has another idea up her sleeve.
