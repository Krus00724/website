Ballanheim is a queer upcycling label from Berlin, run by Andrew, who turns discarded clothes into one-off pieces. I photographed the first campaign, built the identity around a single struck seal and wrote the website, so everything the label puts out is its own.

I met Andrew when he was working a shift at the local Humana in one of his own outfits. The shop is also where a lot of his inspiration comes from, the locals who build a whole look out of worn second-hand pieces.

It took me about a minute to see that the style was not the only thing that was his, the vision was too. Everything he makes is built from recycled material.

When I interviewed him for the website, I learned that his goal is to make something deeply connected to his Scottish heritage and at the same time something rioting against the old scheme of things, which might be quite a common statement in Berlin nowadays.

Clothes, in his opinion, should have no gender, should be as comfortable as it gets and should work on the street as well as on the dancefloor, carrying fresh energy and rediscovered traditional patterns from his home country.

For the campaign he only wanted daylight, and a flash once it got too dark, to keep it raw.

The whole process also taught me how to compromise with a client, and how to run the conversations that get to what they actually want.

bleibt is a brand I made up for Berlin bars: five sodas sold in a heavy glass jar with an aluminium twist lid. The bartender pours 4 cl of spirit up to a line etched into the glass and the long drink is ready. The guest takes the glass home.

People often pick a drink because it looks good and fits their style, more than because it is the best one. In Germany, true fruits sells smoothies in glass bottles and even sells shakers and pourers so people keep the empties. As a kid I loved Voss water for its clear glass cylinder, and I never cared about the water.

Germany has kept mustard jars as drinking glasses since the 19th century. In Poland, where I grew up, that glass even has its own name, musztardówka. bleibt takes the habit and makes the jar the reason to buy.

The timing works. Since 2023, German cafés and bars that sell drinks to go have to offer a reusable option, and in 2025 only 61% of Gen Z in Germany drank alcohol at all. A soda that tastes right with or without the 4 cl fits that crowd, and a glass that stays fits the new rules.

The plan is to sell it by the crate to bars in Neukölln, Kreuzberg and Friedrichshain, to cafés that turn into bars at night and to Spätis. The people who keep the glass are the urban creatives the Sinus Institute calls Expeditives, about one in ten Germans, followed by Performers and the Neo-Ecological milieu.

The jar is 100 mm wide, 82 mm tall and holds 360 ml, shaped like a cosmetics jar so it reads as an object. 200 ml of soda goes in, and the line 60 mm from the base marks the extra 4 cl. The brushed aluminium lid has a seal, so the soda stays fizzy and the drink can leave the bar.

The narrow 28 mm label carries only the flavour, each named after a moment of a Berlin night: Second Date, Raucherraum, Golden Hour, After Hours and Soundcheck. The brand sits on a paper strip across the lid that tears the first time you twist it, so you know nobody has opened the jar before you. The spray-painted grain of the wordmark runs through every label.

The idea came from a conversation with a friend of mine, who is also from Poland. We realised how differently people see the two of us through the lens of gender, and how often she is read as the woman from the East.

So I wanted a slightly messy shoot built on that picture. She is the gangster’s girlfriend, and the gangster turns up too, in a balaclava.

The colours follow the Polish flag, white and red.

She is provocative and free in her poses, and they don’t have to suggest anything other than that she is sexual. Nothing to add, nothing to take away.

I did the styling, the art direction, the photos and the editing.
